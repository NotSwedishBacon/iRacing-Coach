# Shock Dampening

### What is dampening?

Shock dampening helps control the rate at which a spring moves up and down. If shocks did not have any shock dampening the springs would react too quickly and continue to oscillate much longer causing the car to bounce, ultimately making the car very difficult and inconsistent to drive. The more dampening the slower there spring will move and the less dampening the faster the spring will move. So, the shocks help smooth things out for sure, but they also help you control the car balance as explained below.

### Compression vs Rebound

Compression is when the spring and shock get shorter in length and Rebound is when the shock and spring get longer in length.

### Low speed vs high speed dampening

Low speed dampening refers to the amount of dampening the shock provides when the spring is moving up and down at a slow speed. The speed we are talking about here is not the speed of the car but the speed at which the spring/shock is moving up and down. This low speed compression happens for example when you are transitioning weight in the car through things like braking or cornering.

High speed dampening refers to the amount of dampening the shock is providing when the spring is moving up and down at a fast speed. This often happens when you hit a bump or curb.

Although the high speed dampening is only engaged when the spring velocity meets a certain threshold it is important to understand that the high speed dampening is then added to the low speed dampening when the shock velocity is above a threshold (1.5in/s in iRacing). In other words, the level of low speed dampening affects the level of high speed dampening. So, you can't treat them as completely separate from each other. For example, if the low speed dampening is set to 10 and the high speed dampening is set to 4 then when you are just doing normal transitions in cornering, braking or acceleration you are just using the 10 dampening but when you hit a curb or a bump you are using (10+4) 14 dampening.

So, tuning the low speed dampening affects the car in normal driving such as braking, cornering and Acceleration, while high speed dampening in general is tuned to add additional dampening for the curb strikes and bumps of a track.

It is best to keep your high speed compression and rebound settings about the same. For example, if you have too much HS compression and low HS rebound this can result in the suspension expanding too quickly after hitting a bump, and then landing on an overly stiff suspension. . If we look at the opposite scenario where the HS rebound is set really high and the HS compression is set really low, this can produce a suspension that does not return to the it's original state quickly enough (aka as a "locked down suspension"). Both of these examples produce very high load variation at the tire which as mentioned below will reduce mechanical grip.

You will need to explore to determine which HS dampening settings work best for the track you are on but in general the bumpier the track the more HS dampening you will want. If your HS dampening is too low the suspension will collapse too quickly and perhaps hit the track surface. If your HS dampening is too high then your suspension will not compress enough, and the car will just bounce off the bump instead of absorbing them bump.

### Load Variation

Load Variation (the cyclical change in load at the tire contact patch) is also something to keep in mind. A high load variation, meaning the tire cycles between very low and very high loads, is not good for maintaining mechanical grip or a consistent feel in the car. For this reason, you tend to not want to have really high or really low dampening in the car as either extreme can cause high load variations.

### How do shocks affect car balance?

So, the shocks help smooth things out for sure, but they also help you control how fast the bump or rebound happens allowing them to control how fast weight is transferred, which affects the balance of the car.

If we have overall higher low speed dampening, then it will take longer for the springs to be compressed or extend. This does not change the amount of weight transfer, but it does change the rate of the weight transfer. It will take longer to transfer the weight but because the increased low speed dampening will add more roll resistance the weight transfer is greater sooner.

So if you slow down the weight transfer from the inside to the outside tires using more low speed dampening (both compression and rebound) at both the front and rear of the car then this will tend to make the car more consistent and also keep more grip at both ends of the car as it takes longer for the weight to transfer because the shocks are slowing down the rate at which the springs compress. It is also a more linear progression of the weight transfer, so the tires are not as abruptly hit with the extra load, which also helps them keep grip.

So what happens if we use less low speed compression at the front axle and more low speed compression at the rear axle? Since the front will get lower faster than the rear you will have more rake in the car during weight transfer transition which will cause more oversteer.

### Handling Scenarios

In each of the following scenarios, here are things you will help you improve the handling by making changes to front and rear dampeners. You don't need to do all of the suggested changes listed below in these scenarios. It is best to start at the top of the list and do one at a time.

#### Loose Off Power

* Increase front low speed compression
* Decrease rear low speed compression
* Decrease rear low speed rebound
* Increase front low speed rebound

#### Loose On Power

* Decrease rear low speed compression
* Decrease front low speed rebound
* Increase front low speed compression

#### Push Off Power

* Decrease front low speed compression
* Increase rear low speed rebound
* Increase rear low speed compression
* Decrease front low speed rebound

#### Push On Power

* Increase rear low speed compression
* Increase front low speed rebound
* Decrease front low speed compression
